01 · The question

What does an average forecast tell us?

A particle starts at zero. At each step it moves one unit left or one unit right, with equal probability. A deterministic model replaces each random move by its average: zero.

That model predicts the expected position exactly. But how much variation can it hide?

02 · The model and its assumptions

Independent, equally likely steps.

Write the position after n steps as Sn = X1 + ··· + Xn. Each Xi is +1 or −1, each with probability 1/2. The steps are independent, and the initial position is zero.

These assumptions matter. A biased step changes the expected position. Dependence between steps can change the variance calculation.

03 · The calculation

The mean stays put. The uncertainty grows.

Expected positionE[Sn] = 0
VarianceVar(Sn) = n
Standard deviation√n

The average-step approximation captures the mean, while discarding the distribution of possible positions. For example, after two steps the particle is at −2, 0 or 2, with probabilities 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4.

If exactly k of the n moves are to the right, the final position is 2k − n. There are binomial(n, k) such sequences among 2n equally likely sequences. The chart uses those exact probabilities, rather than a random simulation.

05 · Why the calculation works

Three short steps.

  1. Understand one move. E[Xi] = (1/2)(1) + (1/2)(−1) = 0. Also Xi2 = 1, so Var(Xi) = 1.
  2. Add the means. Linearity of expectation gives E[Sn] = 0. This part does not require independence.
  3. Add the variances. Independence makes the covariance terms zero, giving Var(Sn) = 1 + ··· + 1 = n.

Scope: this is an exact calculation for the stated toy model. It establishes no accuracy guarantee for another stochastic process or an investment, consumption or growth model.
