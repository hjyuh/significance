01 · Statement

What is being claimed?

An arithmetic progression has equal gaps: for example, 5, 12, 19, 26 has four terms and common difference 7. A nonconstant progression has positive common difference.

Erdős problem 3 asks whether every set A of positive integers satisfying ∑a∈A 1/a = ∞ contains such progressions of every finite length. The manuscript claims this conclusion as Corollary 1.2.

Its main quantitative claim, Theorem 1.1, concerns rk(N): the largest possible size of a subset of {1,…,N} containing no nonconstant k-term progression. For each fixed integer k ≥ 3, it asserts positive constants Ck, ck, εk such that, for every N ≥ 2,

rk(N) ≤ CkN exp(−ck(log N)εk).

All logarithms are natural. The constants depend on k; this is not a bound with one set of constants uniform in the progression length. The manuscript does not optimize the exponent. Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 1.2, printed p. 4.

02 · Context

Why would this matter?

Positive density means a set occupies a positive proportion of arbitrarily large initial intervals. Szemerédi’s theorem already guarantees arbitrarily long progressions in such sets. Divergence of the reciprocal sum allows much sparser sets: the primes have density zero, but the sum of their reciprocals diverges.

The manuscript’s proposed advance is an all-length bound strong enough to cross a summability threshold. Merely knowing rk(N)/N tends to zero does not cross that threshold. A slowly decreasing quantity can still have a divergent sum when evaluated at N = 2m.

The three-term reciprocal-sum case had already been obtained by Bloom and Sisask; subsequent three-term estimates include Kelley–Meka and further refinements. The manuscript presents its contribution as the summable bound for every fixed length, rather than an improved three-term exponent. Its historical account and bibliography are in §§1.1–1.2, printed pp. 5–6. The literature comparison here follows that account; a specialist has not independently reviewed it for this companion.

For the primes, the existence of arbitrarily long progressions was already proved by Green and Tao. The proposed significance is therefore the general reciprocal-sum criterion, rather than discovering progressions in the primes for the first time.

04 · A deduction the reader can follow

From a finite bound to an infinite conclusion

Assume Theorem 1.1, fix k ≥ 3, and suppose A has no nonconstant k-term progression. Split the positive integers into blocks [2m, 2m+1), for m ≥ 0.

  1. Each block has 2m integers. Translation preserves arithmetic progressions, so it contains at most rk(2m) members of A.
  2. Every reciprocal in that block is at most 2−m. The block’s total reciprocal contribution is therefore at most rk(2m)/2m.
  3. For m ≥ 1, the claimed bound makes this at most Ck exp(−ck(m log 2)εk). Its sum over m converges: for any positive ε and c, c mε eventually exceeds 2 log m, giving a comparison with m−2.

The first block contributes at most 1. Thus the entire reciprocal sum is finite, contradicting the hypothesis. This works separately for each fixed k. Divergence also makes A infinite, which supplies lengths one and two.

This is the short bridge in the manuscript’s proof of Corollary 1.2. It explains why the quantitative claim would suffice; it does not prove Theorem 1.1.

05 · Technical reading guide

The proposed mechanism: density increments with controlled costs

A density-increment argument tries to show that a set without a progression becomes denser inside a smaller structured region. Repeating a fixed multiplicative gain eventually forces density above one. The difficulty is retaining a region large enough for every step to remain available.

The manuscript organizes those regions as triangular polynomial cells: integer variables are constrained successively, with later constraints allowed to depend on earlier variables. Its proposed mechanism keeps inherited equations exact while controlling the number of coordinates and the precision needed for the constraints.

The crucial claimed bookkeeping feature is descending dependence. At a given layer, the extra precision cost depends on higher-layer precisions, rather than that layer’s current precision or lower ones. With a fixed number of layers, the manuscript argues that the total costs remain polynomial in the logarithmic inverse density. This is what its iteration needs for the quasipolynomial threshold.
