How to submit on GitHub

  1. Open the form above. Sign in to GitHub or create an account if prompted.
  2. Give the issue a short title, such as “Record request: Erdős problem 3” or “Correction: source link on [record name].”
  3. Paste the link to the paper, repository, announcement, or existing Significance record.
  4. Select your role: author, reader, or other.
  5. Optionally explain what you want added or corrected. For a correction, identify the section or sentence, suggest the change, and link supporting sources.
  6. Choose Submit new issue. Return to the issue to answer questions or add sources in the comments.

One issue should cover one claim or one focused correction. If you do not know what needs attention, leave the final field blank.

What happens next

  1. A maintainer reads the issue, checks the sources, and asks for missing context when needed.
  2. For a new record, the maintainer applies the publication rules below and prepares a draft of the claim, source version, existing evidence, and open questions. For a correction, they compare the suggested change with the existing record and its sources.
  3. The proposed record or update goes through a pull request, validation, and human review. Attributed findings stay limited to the source version and scope examined.
  4. An accepted change is merged and published in the next successful site deployment. The issue can link to the change; the record keeps a history of updates.

Opening an issue does not automatically publish a record, assign a reviewer, or turn a comment into evidence. A maintainer may close a request without making a record change.

Reply timing

Requests are answered within a few days. That is a promise about the reply, not about the record: the answer may well be that no record will be created, and it will say why. A request that goes nowhere is closed without a public judgement attached to it.

Publication and author participation

Widely circulating public claims from organisations or major announcements may receive editorial records compiled entirely from public sources. The page says plainly that its authors did not participate unless they did.

Ordinary claims by individual living authors are published through an author request or opt-in. A reader may suggest one; maintainers contact the author privately before publication. If the author declines, no record is filed and no decline list is kept.

What to include

Required: link
A link to where it actually lives (paper, preprint, repository, announcement, post). The most stable one, if it is in several places.
Required: your role
Author, reader, or something else. This changes what happens next rather than how the request is ranked: an author can attest to their own work, and a reader asking about someone else's claim starts the contact step above.
Optional: request or correction
A correction, a theorem that matters, or a step you want examined. “Not sure” and a blank answer are both valid.

Want to check a specific step instead?

Go to Open checks, choose a task, and use its question, partial-attempt, or attestation link. A response should describe the exact source, what you checked, and what you found. Use this request form when a record is missing or an existing page needs correcting.

Already received a draft from Significance?

Reply directly to that message with “accurate,” ordinary prose corrections, “keep this private,” or “do not mark this author-confirmed.” No account, schema, or separate form is required. Silence is never represented as approval.

What filing a request does not do

  • It does not commit anyone to creating a record or finding a reviewer.
  • A request that goes nowhere is closed without a public judgement attached to it.
  • A record, if one is created, still asserts nothing about whether the mathematics is right.
